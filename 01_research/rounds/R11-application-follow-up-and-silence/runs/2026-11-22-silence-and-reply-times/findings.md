# Run B findings: How common is employer silence, and how long do replies take?

Run date: 2026-11-22. Desk research on public pages only (11 web searches, 18 page fetches). No logins, accounts, form submissions or messages. Note: the research subagent was not allowed to write files, so this file was saved by the round lead from its returned report.

Evidence labels as in the [round brief](../../round-brief.md): V-site, V-3p, 2nd, I.

Pages already established by R09 G2 and R10 (Employment Hero 41/42 days, Randstad 2017, Robert Half 2017/18, the MCF "typically replies in 30 days" label, the MOM 14 Oct 2025 responsiveness-filter statement) were not redone. One confirmation: the MOM filter statement is now confirmed on the MOM 1014 reply (V-site).

**Method caveat.** The search tool returns model-written summaries, not page text. Summaries were used as leads only. Figures with no fetched page are labelled 2nd or S. Blocked (HTTP 403) and not worked around: Robert Walters pages, Axios, Fast Company, ask.gov.sg/careersatgov. The Indeed SEA report PDF was unreadable, so every Indeed number is second-hand via HCA.

## Findings

**F1. Singapore has no official count of silence, and MOM says it does not collect one.** On 14 Jan 2026 MOM said it "does not collect data on jobseekers receiving updates from potential employers after their interviews" (the question asked about no update beyond 45 days). On 4 Aug 2026 MOM said it does not track complaints about employers not notifying applicants. V-site (MOM). Confidence: high. This is a fact about what MOM collects, not about how common silence is.

**F2. Singapore prevalence figures are weak and self-selected.** The Robert Walters Singapore poll says 88% are "very often" ignored and 13% "sometimes" (sums to 101%), 308 employers and professionals; NTU describes it as a LinkedIn poll, so the sample is mixed or self-selected. Robert Half (500 job seekers, Dec 2017 to Jan 2018): 47% cited slow feedback as the top frustration (already in G2). 2nd (Robert Walters page blocked). Confidence: low. These measure feeling ignored, not a measured no-reply rate.

**F3. Global vendor data gives the clearest rates; definitions vary.** All are source-reported with possible interest.
- Greenhouse platform data, Q2 2024 (release 25 Sep 2024): 31% of candidates never heard back, across 14.4 million applications (V-3p, vendor press release; no method given).
- Same quarter via Axios (2nd, not fetched): only 4% of about 6,500 Greenhouse customers contacted all rejected applicants; 13% ghosted over half of applicants.
- Greenhouse 2024 State of Job Hunting: 61% ghosted after an interview; n=2,500 workers in US, UK, Germany; fieldwork dates not stated (V-3p).
- Criteria Corp 2026: 53% ghosted in the past year (via Fortune, 19 Nov 2026; V-3p of Fortune, 2nd for Criteria).
- Confidence: medium for direction, low for any single number. None is Singapore. Application-level (31%) and respondent-level (53% to 61%) figures measure different things.

**F4. Silence after interviews is reported at a high rate, but only outside Singapore is it quantified.** Greenhouse 61% and the earlier 52% (2024) and 67% (2023) figures come from US-heavy samples (2nd for 52/67). The only Singapore post-interview figures found are anecdotal press stories (not fetched). Confidence: low for Singapore.

**F5. Singapore time-to-reply data exist only for specific employers, mainly government.**
- MOE SEN officer page, updated 11 Nov 2026: applicants "can expect to hear from us within 4 weeks of the close of the application window"; if not heard within 3 weeks after interview, "your application is still being actively considered". Silence is explicitly not rejection there. V-site.
- A search summary reported another MOE page saying that after 6 weeks you should assume you were unsuccessful. That page was not opened, so it is unverified (S).
- Search summaries reported that other agencies (MOE outdoor educator, SID) email unsuccessful applicants and SCDF aims to update "where possible". Summaries only.
- Confidence: medium for the MOE SEN statement, low for the rest.

**F6. LinkedIn publishes a documented reply-speed signal but cannot show it for jobs handled off LinkedIn.** The "review time" estimate is the average review time for at least 35% of applications over 30 days. The badge is hidden if the average exceeds 21 days. "Review" includes a bulk rejection, a profile view or a resume download, not only a human reply. Jobs on an external ATS show "Responses managed off LinkedIn". No markets listed. V-site. Interpretation: a missing badge can mean slow or unmeasured. Confidence: high on definition, none on Singapore reply speed.

**F7. Employer-side delay is rising in Singapore, but no reply-time figure exists.** ManpowerGroup Q4 2026 (released 8 Sep 2026, 651 Singapore employers): 33% said time-to-hire grew versus a year earlier, 42% same, 24% faster. Top reasons for longer timelines: candidate-expectation mismatch (43%), skills shortage (35%). Neither measures replies to candidates. V-3p (HR Online); staffing-vendor survey.

**F8. Candidates leave when the wait stretches.** Indeed 2026 SEA report via HCA (V-3p of HCA, 2nd for Indeed): about 60% in Singapore and Malaysia say excessive interview rounds are a major frustration, and many drop out beyond about 2 to 4 weeks. No sample size or fieldwork date in HCA; Indeed's page says over 4,300 professionals across Singapore, Malaysia and the Philippines. Stated frustration, not observed drop-off by stage. Confidence: low to medium.

**F9. A caution period plausibly adds silence but is not shown to cause it.** SNEF poll (via Vulcan Post, summary only): 58% of 240 firms plan to freeze hiring in 2026, 63% of smaller employers. ManpowerGroup Q3 2026 net employment outlook +13% (summary). Both 2nd. Interpretation, not a measured cause.

**F10. Silence is partly a deliberate policy position, not only an oversight.** MOM (23 Sep 2025) declined to require reasons for rejection, saying it "would be very onerous for employers", citing multiple decision-makers, confidential information on other candidates, limited HR capacity at smaller employers, and application volume at larger ones. It "encourages" constructive feedback "where practical". V-site. Confidence: high.

## Reasons for silence, ranked by strength of evidence

1. **Application volume and limited recruiter capacity.** Strongest, mostly self-reported. MOM names "many applications" and "limited HR capacity" in smaller employers (V-site). Resume Genius: 86% of employers have ghosted because of too many applicants (no method stated, location unclear; V-3p via HCA; weak). A Monster career expert in Fortune (Nov 2026) says recruiters juggle openings, résumé volume and time-to-fill (opinion). Employment Hero (2nd): roles that took longest had more applications. Confidence: medium.
2. **Hiring decisions involve other people and confidential information, so employers avoid feedback.** MOM 23 Sep 2025 (V-site). This explains no reasons given, not necessarily no outcome. Confidence: high that MOM states it.
3. **No obligation to notify.** MOM and TAFEP frame notification as encouraged or a courtesy; no complaint tracking, no penalty. Interpretation: removes pressure to reply. Confidence: medium.
4. **ATS auto-flows without rejection emails.** Greenhouse's 4% (2nd) shows few employers on one platform contact all rejected applicants. One summary reported stale ATS rejection templates (unnamed talent director; unverified). Confidence: low to medium.
5. **Ghost or evergreen postings.** Greenhouse classes 18 to 22% of postings as ghost jobs (2024 State of Job Hunting via Greenhouse blog; definition not on the page). MOM (14 Oct 2025): posting the same role on several portals is normal and does not by itself mean a fake opening; MOM validates reported vacancies against MCF postings and follows up on repeat unfilled roles (V-site). No Singapore ghost-job rate found. Confidence: low in Singapore.
6. **Hiring freezes and uncertainty.** SNEF and ManpowerGroup show a cautious market (2nd). No source ties freezes to non-reply. Interpretation. Confidence: low.
7. **Internal candidates and legal-risk avoidance.** Some blogs argue lawyers advised against written rejections; others dispute it (2nd, commentary). No Singapore source. Internal candidates: no source found. Confidence: low.
8. **Counter-evidence: silence is not free for employers.** A Dutch-Belgian study (236 adults, Feb 2026 per Fortune) found a generic rejection lowered employer attractiveness while an appreciative one did not. Greenhouse describes an AI-drafted rejection cutting drafting time from 20 to 30 minutes to under 3 (Fortune; vendor-sourced).

## Singapore rules and guidance on notifying unsuccessful candidates

- **Tripartite Standard on Recruitment Practices (TSRP).** TAFEP lists "Unsuccessful candidates are informed of the outcome of the interview" as a practice for adopters. Adoption is voluntary. It also asks employers to keep interview records for at least one year. V-site. 6,620 entities adopted as of end May 2026, including government agencies (MOM reply, 4 Aug 2026).
- **TAFEP, Conducting Job Interviews.** Telling candidates how results will be communicated, and informing them "even if they are unsuccessful", is a courtesy in the "progressive" section, based on the TSRP, separate from the mandatory "What You Must Do to Be Fair" list. V-site, undated (2025 footer).
- **MOM, 14 Jan 2026.** MOM "encourages employers to inform candidates during the interview on how the interview results will be communicated" and to notify within a reasonable timeframe. No timeframe set. V-site.
- **MOM, 4 Aug 2026.** Does not track complaints about employers not notifying applicants. V-site.
- **MOM, 23 Sep 2025.** Declined to mandate reasons to unsuccessful interviewees; encourages feedback "where practical"; points to the Workplace Fairness Act for recourse on discrimination. V-site.
- **Workplace Fairness Act.** Summaries and a Singapore Law Watch headline say it is expected to take effect around end-2027 after passing on 4 Nov 2025. Article text not read; date not confirmed from MOM or the statute. One summary says it covers recruitment discrimination. No source found saying it requires notifying candidates.

No current Singapore legal requirement for an employer to reply to an applicant was found. This is a null on the pages read, not proof that none exists.

## What could NOT be found

- Singapore days to first response after applying from any named survey (matches the G2 gap).
- Singapore share of applications that never get a reply, measured rather than polled.
- Stage-by-stage drop-off for Singapore or Asia-Pacific.
- Jobstreet, Randstad Workmonitor, Hays, Michael Page and Glassdoor candidate-side reply figures; Robert Walters pages were blocked.
- The method behind MCF "typically replies in N days" and what counts as a reply; no MCF help page defining it.
- Whether MOM's responsiveness filter has launched, and its definition.
- Reply behaviour split by employer size or type (SMEs vs large, agencies vs direct, shift or gig). Closest: MOM says smaller employers have limited HR capacity; SNEF says smaller firms are more likely to freeze (63%). Neither measures replies.
- Indeed SEA report PDF text; Singapore-only slice of its numbers.
- Careers@Gov's central rule on notifying unsuccessful applicants (FAQ blocked).
- Statutory text of the Workplace Fairness Act on recruitment and commencement.

## Limitations and assumptions

- WebSearch summaries are leads only. Several contradicted each other or the pages (for example the MOE "6 weeks" line, and a summary denying the MCF "typically replies" label that R10 saw directly). The R10 reading stands over the summary.
- Every vendor and recruiter figure is source-reported, with a possible interest.
- Greenhouse and most "ghosted" figures are US-led: global context, not Singapore.
- "Ghosted" is defined differently in each source; denominators differ (per application, per candidate, per past year, per interview).
- Stated frustration and stated intent to drop out are not observed behaviour.
- Desk research cannot show real reply behaviour; that needs the interview and diary test (N3).
- Employer conduct was not inferred from missing information alone.

## Implications for the FEAT-012 candidate approaches (observations, not decisions)

1. **Plain status list the user updates.** Supported: MOM does not track outcomes and employers have no duty to notify, so the user is the only party who will know the status. Statuses vary by portal; LinkedIn's "review" counts actions other than a reply. Unknown: whether users keep a list at all (run A).
2. **Expected-wait signal.** Partial support: MCF shows "typically replies in N days" and LinkedIn publishes a review-time badge. Against: LinkedIn hides it over 21 days or when unmeasured, MCF's method is undocumented, and there is no Singapore reply-time dataset. Any signal would have to be quoted from MCF, LinkedIn or the employer's own page; no JobHub-owned source exists. Employer-stated waits exist for government (MOE) but no equivalent for corporates, SMEs or shift employers was found.
3. **Give-up-after-N-days rule.** Supported only as a user-set number: no published norm, MOM sets no timeframe, and MOE says silence at 3 weeks after interview still means active consideration. Indeed's 2 to 4 week window is about candidates leaving, not employers replying. A default that implies "silence means no" would contradict MOE's own statement; for the Responsible AI Reviewer to weigh against "never invent claims".
4. **Follow-up templates the user sends.** Whether follow-up works is for run C. Relevant here: feedback is encouraged "where practical", and employers cite capacity limits, so templates should not assume a reply will come.
5. **Closure step (user records "no reply" and moves on).** Supported: nobody closes the loop for the user (MOM does not track, notification is voluntary, Greenhouse's 4%). No evidence here on whether users want it.

**Cross-cutting.** Strongest evidence: silence exists and is tolerated by policy. Weakest: how long a wait is normal in Singapore and how it differs by employer type. A JobHub expected-wait number would need a source it can name, or user-recorded data collected later.
