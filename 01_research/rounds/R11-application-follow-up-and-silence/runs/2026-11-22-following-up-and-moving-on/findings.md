# Run C findings: What job seekers do, and what works, when an employer does not reply

Run date: 2026-11-22. Role: Job-Seeker Practices Researcher (#04). Read-only desk research on public pages (about 14 searches, about 30 page fetches). No logins, accounts, form submissions or messages. Note: the research subagent was not allowed to write files, so this file was saved by the round lead from its returned report. R09 G2 was not redone.

Evidence labels as in the [round brief](../../round-brief.md).

**Fetched and read in full (V-3p):** Jobstreet Singapore (4 articles), Indeed Singapore, the NTU reprint of the Straits Times piece, e2i (2019), Wood et al. 2023 (abstract page), Workforce.com (Accountemps 2017), Fortune (19 Nov 2026), CPA Practice Advisor (2 pieces on the Resume Genius 2026 report), the MOM parliamentary reply (2021), MustShareNews (SUSS study).

**Fetch failures (not bypassed):** two MyCareersFuture CareersCompass pages (2020 interview follow-up, 2018 "Didn't get the job") returned only titles and metadata; several MCF `?p=NNN` URLs resolved to the landing page; The Independent SG and Britcham (Robert Walters) returned 403 and are cited from snippets only; Jobstreet community threads rendered no posts; the Dev.to follow-up experiment returned 404 (snippet only). No e2i, NTUC LHUB, WSG or MCF career-service page giving readable follow-up guidance was found.

## Findings

| # | Finding | Label | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Singapore career advice converges on one follow-up after applying at about 1 to 2 weeks (Jobstreet), and warns against following up within about 3 days. Fast-moving industries may follow up sooner. | V-3p | high that this is the advice, low that it is validated |
| F2 | After an interview, Jobstreet (Jan 2026) says to ask about the timeline in the interview and send a thank-you the same evening or next day, then a status follow-up 3 to 5 days later. Another Jobstreet piece (updated 18 Nov 2026) says 5 to 10 business days; another (2022) says 1 to 2 weeks. Advice for the same stage varies from 3 days to 2 weeks. | V-3p | high that the advice is inconsistent |
| F3 | When to stop is advised only loosely. Jobstreet: if nothing for about 2 weeks even after a polite follow-up, move on; a "final follow-up" template exists. A search summary of MCF says "a month after application and follow-up email" (page not read; another summary attributed the month rule to a non-MCF Singapore article). | V-3p (Jobstreet), 2nd (MCF month rule) | medium |
| F4 | Email is the default channel in Singapore advice. Phone is a fallback when no email exists, or for small or local firms. Jobstreet says in-person is rare. Indeed lists LinkedIn and company social media as alternatives. One search result said recruiters avoid WhatsApp as first contact (unverified). | V-3p / 2nd | medium |
| F5 | The recruiter view is that replying to everyone is unrealistic. The Straits Times quotes a recruiter managing about 10 postings and speaking to 30+ applicants per role. NTU and SMU academics attribute ghosting to application volume (easy-apply), the labour-market balance of power, and no process for updating rejected applicants. A Hays consultant says applicants deserve updates and her firm tries to close the loop. | V-3p (NTU reprint) | medium; quotes, not data |
| F6 | **No evidence found that following up changes response or hire rates.** No audit or field experiment on status-inquiry follow-ups. All "it works" claims are advice, a self-experiment, or a vendor claim. | I (not found) | medium |
| F7 | An experiment with 554 unemployed people found no evidence that being ghosted is worse than being rejected on psychological needs (belonging, self-esteem, meaningful existence, control). Ghosted participants reported somewhat higher self-esteem and control than those given personal feedback. Authors caution long-term effects are unstudied. The task was a hypothetical application, so it says little about real prolonged silence. | V-3p (abstract page) | medium for the finding, low for generalising |
| F8 | US survey of 1,000 active job seekers (Resume Genius, via Pollfish): 55% named "no response after applying" and 44% post-interview silence as top frustrations; 49% said job hunting hurts their mental health; not hearing back (46%) and rejections (47%) were top contributors. | V-3p (secondary reporting of a vendor survey) | medium; US, vendor |
| F9 | A Tilburg experiment (236 working adults and students in the Netherlands and Belgium, hypothetical letters, via Fortune) found a generic rejection lowered employer attractiveness while an appreciative one did not. Fortune also reports US vendor surveys where about half would not reapply after being ghosted. Candidates say a reply of any kind helps them redirect effort. | V-3p (Fortune; studies not read) | low to medium; non-Singapore |
| F10 | Singapore anecdotes show the toll: a 29-year-old designer applied to 130+ jobs in 6 months and said it "felt like I wasn't good enough to get a reply" (Straits Times via NTU); a 58-year-old e2i client felt discouraged after two years without work, and his coach found the cause was missing skills and a thin network, not age. | V-3p, anecdote | low as population evidence |
| F11 | MOM said in 2021 that MCF application counts and success counts are "not meaningful" to compare, because people apply across many platforms. No employer-response data on that page. | V-3p | high |
| F12 | Recruiter surveys on the follow-up debate are US and old: an Accountemps survey (2017, no sample given) reports 81% of senior managers want a follow-up within 2 weeks, 64% prefer email, 21% phone. It does not measure harm from over-chasing. | V-3p (Workforce.com) | low |

## Does following up work? Evidence separated from advice

**Evidence (observed or measured):**
- No study measures whether a status follow-up changes callback or hire rates (F6).
- A 554-person experiment: ghosting was not clearly worse than rejection on psychological needs (F7).
- The Tilburg experiment (via Fortune): rejection wording changes employer perception. About employer brand, not candidate follow-up.
- A South African audit study found reference letters raise employer response (4.2% to 6.7%). Not read, snippet-level, not about follow-ups.
- Accountemps survey: hiring managers say they want follow-ups (2017, US, stated preference).
- Jobstreet says a follow-up "won't necessarily change the outcome" (V-3p), an honest caveat in an otherwise advice-giving source.

**Advice or anecdote only:**
- All timing norms above.
- A Dev.to self-experiment (about 40 follow-ups; a day-7 nudge reportedly beat a day-2 nudge): snippet-only, uncontrolled, fetch failed.
- "3x response" template claims from a vendor blog (snippet-only), no stated method.
- Jobstreet PH says 80% of recruiters say thank-you emails influence decisions (snippet, source unnamed).
- Evidence of harm (seen as pushy) is also only advice ("avoid desperation", "one or two attempts"). No measurement of harm found.

## How people decide to move on, and emotional impact

- Advice sets arbitrary limits: about 2 weeks after a follow-up (Jobstreet) or about a month (MCF, 2nd). Rules vary because no data supports a threshold.
- SMU academic advice in the NTU reprint: keep applying and do not wait for a reply.
- None of the sources read describe how people actually decide. The Independent SG pieces about a 3-week wait after four interview rounds ("at what point is the silence a no") would be closest but returned 403 (titles only).
- Emotional toll evidence is mostly anecdotal or vendor-based (F8, F10). The one controlled study (F7) is a hypothetical task. Real prolonged silence, self-blame and motivation decay in Singapore are not measured by anything found. A search for self-blame specifically returned nothing.
- Indeed suggests coping by rehearsing interview questions. e2i's coach reframed rejections as skill and network gaps, an example of re-attributing silence away from self-blame.

**What people wish employers or tools would tell them (F9, F5):** a reply at all, even brief, so they can redirect effort; personal acknowledgement for finalists. The Resume Genius article did not say directly what seekers want (inference). No recent independent survey asks this directly. One Fortune-reported study found 48% would not reapply to a company that ghosted them (US vendor, 1,008 workers).

## Differences by user group

- **Early-career:** anecdote-level only (NTU 130-application case; Independent SG graduate pieces as snippets). The SUSS study (1,040 youths, Jun to Jul 2025): 68% actively or passively looking, 35% of those aged 18 to 25 actively searching; the no-reply point is a single anecdote. Nothing shows early-career people follow up differently.
- **Mid-career switchers:** the e2i profile (2019, one person): silence interpreted as age until a coach reframed it as skill and network gaps. Forum and news snippets describe many applications with few replies but could not be read. Interpretation: switchers may need a gap diagnosis more than a follow-up nudge.
- **Lower-wage, shift and gig workers:** no source on follow-up norms. Jobstreet and MCF advice assumes email and professional-role hiring. US audit studies were about callbacks, not follow-up. A real gap.

## What could NOT be found

- Any experiment or large sample showing follow-up changes response or hire rates, in any country.
- Evidence of harm from following up, beyond advice and an old US stated-preference survey.
- Readable follow-up guidance from e2i, NTUC LHUB, WSG or a university career office (NUS results were only advisor booking and a workshop listing; MCF follow-up article bodies did not load).
- Singapore data on how people decide to give up, or how often they follow up in practice.
- Singapore evidence on WhatsApp, phone and walk-in follow-up, and anything for gig, shift or lower-wage workers.
- A sample size for the Robert Walters 88% figure (the NTU reprint gives none; a 308 sample from another summary is unconfirmed).
- Singapore self-blame or motivation-drop measurement.
- The WSG-coach "final email after two weeks" quote at source (a search summary, likely via The Independent SG); unverified.

## Limitations and assumptions

- Desk research cannot show real behaviour; conclusions capped at N2.
- Most career-advice sources are platforms or recruiters with content-marketing interests, and Jobstreet's own pages disagree with each other.
- MCF follow-up article bodies could not be read; claims about its 5 to 10 business day and 1-month guidance rest on summaries, one of which attributed the month rule to a different source.
- Indeed SG advice is assumed generic rather than Singapore-specific; Singapore forum behaviour is assumed similar to the Independent SG cases, which could not be read.
- Fortune's reporting of the Tilburg and other surveys is second-hand; the studies were not read.

## Implications for the FEAT-012 candidate approaches (observations, not decisions)

1. **Plain status list.** No evidence tests this. It addresses the confirmed pain, since silence is the top frustration in the US survey and several Singapore anecdotes. It cannot provide the information employers withhold (S-20). The 130-application anecdote suggests tracking load in high-volume search, but no data.
2. **Expected-wait signal.** Singapore hiring-time figures read are vague. MCF's 30-day "typically replies" label (R10) and the 25.4-day interview process figure (unverified) are not comparable. Post-interview advice ranges from 3 days to 2 weeks and no source separates by employer type. Any single number would risk false precision; a signal built from advice ranges would be a rule of thumb, not data.
3. **Give-up-after-N-days rule.** Advice offers about 2 weeks after follow-up (Jobstreet) or about a month (MCF, 2nd); neither is validated and no source shows when a reply stops being likely. A user-set N fits that uncertainty better than a fixed default. The ghosting experiment (F7) is not strong enough to claim closing an application reduces distress.
4. **Follow-up templates the user sends.** Most directly supported by what Singapore career sites already publish: short, polite emails naming the role and application date, restating a few strengths, asking about timing, thanking the reader, with a final follow-up variant. Email is the dominant channel, so a phone-first user group may not fit. No evidence follow-ups raise reply rates, so a template feature should not imply it does. JobHub not sending messages fits the advice to keep each follow-up personal and one-off.
5. **Closure step.** The Fortune-reported material and the recruiter view suggest closure is valued, and e2i's reframing is one case of moving on usefully. No evidence recording "no reply" improves outcomes or mood. It would feed FEAT-004. Unknown whether users want to mark closure at all, which suits the planned interview and diary test.
6. **Not served by any approach:** lower-wage, shift and gig workers. Nothing found on their follow-up channels or needs, so the user test should include them before deciding.
