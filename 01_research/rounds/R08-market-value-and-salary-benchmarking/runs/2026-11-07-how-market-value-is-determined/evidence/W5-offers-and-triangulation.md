# W5: how employers set offers and how a job seeker triangulates (raw agent note)

*Haiku-model research agent, 2026-11-07. Saved unedited by the lead researcher. Not yet checked; see `findings.md` for what was confirmed.*

A) FINDINGS

1. Compa-ratio is an employee's pay divided by the range midpoint; 1.0 (100%) means paid at midpoint. [https://www.hrsource.org/MAIMIS/HRS/Articles/2024/10/29/Compa-Ratio__Position-in-Range.aspx] [SECONDARY]

2. Range penetration (position in range) is (salary minus range minimum) divided by (maximum minus minimum), so 50% sits at the midpoint. The same HR Source article gives both formulas. [https://www.hrsource.org/MAIMIS/HRS/Articles/2024/10/29/Compa-Ratio__Position-in-Range.aspx] [SECONDARY]

3. Range spread has no single definition. AIHR's glossary defines it relative to the minimum, (max minus min) divided by min, with midpoint-relative variants also in use. [https://www.aihr.com/hr-glossary/range-spread/] [SECONDARY]

4. A pay band is built around a midpoint, which is the target for a fully competent worker. The minimum is the typical starting pay and the maximum is the ceiling for highly experienced staff. Adjacent bands may overlap, and each higher band should have a clearly higher midpoint. Market data should set the ranges. [https://workleap.com/blog/guide-to-building-pay-bands] [SECONDARY]

5. WorldatWork says many companies pay at the median (50th percentile), with high performers sometimes targeted at the 75th or 90th. Positioning should weigh company stability, talent supply and productivity, be based on current survey data, be benchmarked every one to two years, and be documented against the compensation philosophy. [https://worldatwork.org/publications/workspan-daily/how-to-set-proper-market-pay-rates-for-your-sales-roles] [SECONDARY]

6. BLS OEWS publishes wage percentiles at the 10th, 25th, median, 75th and 90th levels, at national, state and metro level. The latest reference is May 2025. It does not break wages out by experience. [https://www.bls.gov/oes/earnings.htm] [PRIMARY]

7. A job-seeker guide recommends checking at least three sources (Glassdoor, LinkedIn Salary, Indeed) filtered by metro area and experience level, and comparing medians and 75th percentiles. It also says to check two to three recent postings for the same title. [https://www.loopcv.pro/guides/how-to-research-salary-for-a-job/] [PRACTICE]

8. The same guide suggests aiming between the 50th and 75th percentile, with the 25th as a floor. This differs from the median default employers often use (see B). [https://www.loopcv.pro/guides/how-to-research-salary-for-a-job/] [PRACTICE]

9. In a 2011 study (Thorsteinson, Journal of Applied Social Psychology), about 206 students acting as hiring managers gave higher starting salaries after seeing an extreme anchor of $100,000 (also a joking $1 anchor was tested). A second experiment with a $1 million anchor also raised offers. [https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/extreme-numbers-influence-initial-salary-offers] [SECONDARY]

10. A 2025 meta-analysis (Petrowsky et al., Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes), as reported in a secondary article, finds the first mover gets better economic outcomes in single-issue negotiations such as salary. Moving first is riskier when you know less than the other side. [https://www.mindsetonline.com/how-the-first-number-in-any-negotiation-controls-the-outcome/] [SECONDARY]

11. The same secondary article reports that offering a range can outperform a single number, citing Harvard's Program on Negotiation. I did not verify this at the primary source. [https://www.mindsetonline.com/how-the-first-number-in-any-negotiation-controls-the-outcome/] [SECONDARY]

12. In a field experiment with about 2,500 job seekers (Leibbrandt and List, NBER WP 18511, 2012; Management Science 2015), men negotiated more than women when wages were not stated as negotiable. When negotiability was stated, the gap disappeared and tended to reverse. Men also preferred jobs with ambiguous wage rules, and the gap was largest there. [https://www.nber.org/papers/w18511] [PRIMARY]

13. Total rewards is the mix of pay, benefits and other incentives. Its components include base pay, bonuses and commissions, equity, health insurance, retirement plans with employer matches, and paid time off. The article notes benefits can decide whether a candidate accepts, but it does not explain how to compare offers. [https://www.paycom.com/resources/blog/total-rewards/] [SECONDARY]

B) WHERE SOURCES DISAGREE OR ARE UNCLEAR

- Target percentile: WorldatWork's article says many firms pay at the median, but the guide in claim 8 advises 50th to 75th for the candidate. Both are practice views, not measured outcomes.
- Spread: AIHR gives a min-relative formula, while other sources (seen only in search snippets) use midpoint-relative definitions. Check which convention a company uses before comparing bands.
- Negotiation effects: the Leibbrandt and List working paper says the gap reverses when wages are negotiable; the published abstract is worded more strongly. Treat the reversal claim with care.
- Anchoring: the 2025 meta-analysis and the range-offer advantage are known only through a secondary article.
- Stated versus actual positioning (a company says 75th, but its quotas imply the median) was seen only in a search snippet, not in an opened page.
