## Proposed next study: bounded episode reconstruction

**Proposal only; not authorised for execution by this desk-research approval.** This is a concrete option for a later human research decision, not a recruitment instruction. No participants have been approached. Sample size, participation support, privacy/consent arrangements and final study scope remain for that decision. Completing the present local artifacts needs no additional approval.

Propose eight adults searching in Singapore, each with at least one concrete application or considered role in the preceding eight weeks. Seek variation across the four approved audience contexts, aiming for two participants associated with each while allowing overlapping identities. Include at least three people pursuing service, trades or operational work; include assisted/offline routes where possible. These are coverage goals to challenge the corpus bias, not representative quotas or a target-segment choice. Record unmet coverage rather than substituting more easily reached graduate professionals and claiming completion across groups. Establish return intention and current availability before classifying someone as an active seeker or returner.

Use one 45–60 minute interview per person. Reconstruct up to two recent episodes: preferably one pursued or progressed and one stopped or declined. If that contrast is unavailable, document the limitation; never require a manufactured example. Invite an optional participant-led walkthrough of a redacted artifact or usual process, without asking for account credentials or submitting an application. Participants may choose narration alone. Any later study must settle consent, retention and accessible participation arrangements before collection; this proposal makes no privacy-policy decision.

Ask neutral episode questions before introducing the hypotheses:

1. What triggered this search, and what made this role worth considering? What other demands or constraints mattered at that time?
2. What did you do next, in what order, with whose help? Where did you pause or return to a task?
3. What information did you have at each decision? When and how did you learn a requirement or condition that changed your choice?
4. What did you prepare or repeat, how long did it take, and why? What changed between the two episodes?
5. What happened after submission or assessment? What feedback was available, and what did you actually do in response?
6. What, if anything, would you have done differently with information that could reasonably have been available earlier? What evidence supports that answer?

For each stage, record active minutes as a range, elapsed waiting separately, number/type of repeated tasks, whether work was reused, interruptions/help, and the actual next action. Mark each observation as artifact-supported, participant recall, researcher interpretation or unknown. Timestamps establish elapsed intervals, not active effort. Collect optional anchored self-reports of confidence and emotional effort at the episode's decision points; retrospective ratings are exploratory, not validated change measures. Record a concrete missed or declined action separately from a conjectured lost opportunity. Do not combine these dimensions into an invented priority score.

Code conditions/exclusion, information uncertainty and process effort separately, allowing overlap. Record alternative explanations and negative cases alongside supporting episodes. Report counts with their small purposive denominator only to describe this sample. Within-person contrasts can improve questions but cannot yield population rankings or causal effects.

## Disconfirmation, stopping and changes of direction

| Question | Evidence that would weaken the proposed mechanism or change direction |
| --- | --- |
| H1 | If repetition is mostly purposeful tailoring or practice, or persists despite clear feedback, weaken the feedback-causes-rework explanation. If needed information is inherently unavailable, do not propose a system that invents it. Seek the actual decision obstacle instead. |
| H2 | If assessment effort is modest by participants' own accounts, knowingly accepted, reusable or reasonably necessary, do not advance an avoidable-burden claim. A small sample with no relevant episode leaves occurrence uncertain; it does not prove absence. |
| H3 | If decisive conditions are known early and genuinely incompatible, treat the issue as a feasibility constraint, with product benefit unestablished. If a misunderstood or late-discovered condition changes a documented choice, that supports a narrower information question for later testing. |

After the first four interviews, review whether episodes are recent and specific enough to reconstruct and whether the planned variation is being achieved. Revise the guide for ambiguity rather than steering toward confirmation. If most accounts cannot distinguish active work from waiting or explain actual decisions, stop treating retrospective interviews as sufficient measurement and propose a short prospective diary before extending the same method. Do not automatically commence that diary.

Stop after eight completed interviews and one cross-case review. This cap does not imply thematic saturation. Return an episode matrix, supported/unsupported mechanisms, negative cases, coverage gaps and a proposed next decision. Further recruitment requires an explicit reason tied to a remaining uncertainty. Consider a small prototype only if a concrete interaction uncertainty emerges and the human selects it for testing; this research proposal creates no must-build requirement.

## Risks, trade-offs and review disposition

Small varied samples improve the chance of detecting assumptions that fail across contexts, but provide little depth within any one group. Recent episodes and optional artifacts reduce some recall uncertainty while creating participation and privacy considerations. Hypothesis-led questions risk confirmation bias; begin with open reconstruction and retain counterexamples. Distress, unemployment circumstances and caregiving details are not necessary in greater detail than the research question requires.
