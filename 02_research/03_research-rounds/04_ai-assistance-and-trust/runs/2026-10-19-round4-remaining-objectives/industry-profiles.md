# Industry and employment-context profiles

## Core finding

Job fit cannot be reduced to a generic keyword or skills score. The four contexts studied differ in work setting, hiring channel, credentials, employment arrangement, physical or social conditions and the evidence available for market value.

## Profiles

### Agriculture, aquaculture and food production

Singapore’s agri-food ecosystem includes farm operations, aquaculture, food production, engineering, ICT, automation, food safety and data/production work. SFA’s Skills Framework provides role families, tasks, skills and pathways; its recruitment guidance also separates farming operations from technical and agritech work.

Fit evidence may include husbandry or production experience, water quality, equipment, food safety, biology, engineering, ICT and resilience. A technical background may support agritech systems without proving hands-on husbandry competence; practical farming experience may be valuable without formal credentials.

Key unknowns are vacancy volume, resident/migrant composition, physical and shift conditions, and comparable pay by role. A selected farmer transition story is useful as a bounded example, not a typical-outcome estimate.

### Utilities, energy, water and waste

This is not one “green sector.” It includes regulated technical roles, plant and field operations, public infrastructure, water systems, waste collection, materials recovery, pest management and transformed frontline work. EMA, PUB and NEA materials describe structured skills, training and career pathways. Waste management has unusually explicit Progressive Wage Model job levels, training requirements and wage floors for covered roles.

Fit evidence may include licences, engineering or equipment competence, safety, incident response, route knowledge, reliability, WSQ training, digital skills and sustainability knowledge. Formal pathways indicate possible progression, not guaranteed progression.

### Maritime, offshore and seafaring

MPA distinguishes shipboard from shore-based work and covers seafaring, ports, shipping, marine engineering, commercial operations, digitalisation, cybersecurity and sustainability. Shipboard roles can have hard gates: STCW-aligned training, certificates/endorsements, sea service and medical fitness.

Fit must distinguish deck, engine, electro-technical, cargo, safety, port, commercial, technical and shore-based roles. A generic “maritime experience” label is insufficient. Public workforce material also records deterrents such as sea time, difficult conditions, remuneration concerns and long progression timelines.

### Domestic and household services

Singapore has distinct arrangements: directly employed live-in migrant domestic workers and company-mediated Household Services Scheme work. Household work can combine cleaning, cooking, childcare, elder-minding and other duties; basic intermittent elder-minding is not the same as specialised care.

Fit includes task bundle, care complexity, language, experience, training, household routine, live-in or part-time status, rest, privacy and supervision expectations. A large MOM survey and a small qualitative study provide different views of experience; neither alone establishes prevalence. Matching quality includes lived conditions and power dynamics, not only task competence.

## Role-fit model implied by the evidence

For any context, the evidence suggests separating:

1. **Hard gates** — legal, credential, medical, licence or explicit eligibility conditions.
2. **Strong signals** — demonstrated tasks, equipment, environment or sector experience.
3. **Transferable bridges** — prior skill, target task, evidence of transfer and the gap-closing step.
4. **Trainable gaps** — skills that can plausibly be acquired through training or supervised work.
5. **Preferences and constraints** — pay, shifts, location, deployment, physical conditions, care duties, privacy and progression.
6. **Unknown or conflicting information** — never silently completed from a title or similar posting.

## Market-value evidence

Market value should be described as scoped labour-market evidence, not personal worth. Useful dimensions include occupational wage benchmarks, legal wage floors, scarcity/demand signals, credential premiums, progression pathways, employer-specific pay and non-wage conditions. MOM’s occupational wage tables are a benchmark for full-time resident employees and exclude bonuses; waste PWM is more role-specific but limited to covered roles and worker categories.

No single score is justified by this research. Pay, suitability, safety, dignity, stability, family constraints and progression can conflict and should remain visible as separate considerations.
